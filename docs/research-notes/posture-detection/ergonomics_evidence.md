# Clinical & Ergonomic Evidence on Seated Posture, Movement and Breaks — What a Desk Posture-Coach App Should Detect and Say

> Access note for the report writer: PubMed, Cochrane.org, Springer, SJWEH, Cornell and most publisher sites were blocked by this environment's network proxy, so findings below come from search-engine extractions of abstracts, institutional repositories, and (where flagged) secondary summaries. Numbers were only recorded when they appeared explicitly in a retrieved result; secondary/non-peer-reviewed sources are labelled as such. Citation correction: the "Sit up straight" editorial by Slater et al. 2019 appeared in **JOSPT 49(8):562–564**, not BJSM.

## 1. Is there an "ideal" or "correct" sitting posture? (slouched vs upright sitting and low back pain)

### Takeaway
No posture has been shown to be "correct" in the sense of preventing pain. Umbrella and causal-assessment reviews find no consensus that spinal posture or sitting per se causes low back pain (LBP), people with and without LBP do not differ in lordosis or standing pelvic tilt, and expert editorials (Slater 2019 JOSPT; O'Sullivan 2020 BJSM) conclude that there is no single optimal posture and that comfortable postures vary between people. The office-worker associations that do exist are mostly cross-sectional, and they point more to *static sitting behaviour* than to any particular spinal shape.

### Cited Findings
- **Swain et al. 2020 (J Biomech 102:109312), a review of 41 systematic reviews** (searched 1990–June 2018; appraised with AMSTAR and the Bradford Hill causality criteria). The included reviews reported both positive and null associations for spine posture, prolonged standing, sitting, bending/twisting, awkward postures, whole-body vibration and heavy physical work. Meta-analyses were more consistently in favour of an association, but reviews restricted to prospective studies were "less able to provide consistent conclusions". Conclusion: "there is no consensus regarding causality of physical exposure to LBP." — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8wq8w/no-consensus-on-causality-of-spine-postures-or-physical-exposure-and-low-back-pain-a-systematic-review-of-systematic-reviews); [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/no-consensus-on-causality-of-spine-postures-or-physical-exposure-/); [PubMed 31451200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31451200/)
- **Roffey et al. 2010 (Spine J 10(3):252–61), causal assessment of occupational sitting using Bradford Hill criteria.** Found strong and consistent evidence of *no* association between occupational sitting and LBP, and reasonable evidence of no dose–response relationship. A companion review on occupational standing/walking (Spine J 10(3):262–72) reached a similar conclusion. Secondary summary; primary not accessible. — [IASP Relief News summary](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/relief-news/article/back-pain-myths-closing-sale-everything-must-go/)
- **Laird et al. 2014 (BMC Musculoskelet Disord 15:229), systematic review and meta-analysis.** No difference in lordosis angle (8 studies) and no difference in standing pelvic tilt between people with and without LBP. The differences that did appear in people with LBP were reduced lumbar range of motion (ROM), slower movement and reduced proprioception. — [PMC4096432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4096432/)
- **Slater, Korakakis, O'Sullivan K, O'Sullivan P, Nolan 2019 (JOSPT 49(8):562–564), "Sit Up Straight: Time to Re-evaluate".** Key points as extracted:
  - there is no strong evidence that one optimal posture exists, or that avoiding "incorrect" postures prevents LBP;
  - spinal curves vary naturally, and no single curvature is strongly associated with pain;
  - comfortable postures vary between individuals;
  - the spine is robust and adaptable;
  - common warnings to protect the spine are not evidence-informed and can lead to fear;
  - postural and movement screening does not prevent workplace pain.

  — [UL Pure record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/sit-up-straight-time-to-re-evaluate/)
- **Korakakis et al. 2019 (Musculoskelet Sci Pract), survey of 544 Greek physiotherapists.** 93.9% rated posture education "considerably" or "very" important. Three variants of upright lordotic sitting accounted for 97.5% of the "optimal sitting" choices, and slouched or forward-head postures were almost never chosen. Respondents used the same justifications ("natural curves", "muscle activation") whichever spinal configuration they chose. The belief in an "ideal posture" is therefore widespread among clinicians but inconsistently justified. — [UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/physiotherapist-perceptions-of-optimal-sitting-and-standing-postu/); [Physio Network review](https://www.physio-network.com/review/physiotherapist-perceptions-of-optimal-sitting-and-standing-posture)
- **O'Sullivan P et al. 2020 (BJSM 54(12):698), "Back to basics: 10 facts every person should know about back pain".** Fact 6: "Back pain is not caused by poor posture." Fact 7: "Back pain is not caused by a 'weak core'." The editorial presents the facts as counters to unhelpful beliefs that shape behavioural and psychological responses to pain. — [BJSM full text](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/12/698.full)
- **Dankaerts et al. (Spine 2006; classification model 2009).** Subgroups of non-specific chronic LBP behave in *opposite* directions:
  - "flexion pattern" patients had *less* trunk muscle activity in usual sitting than controls;
  - "active extension pattern" patients had *hyperactive* back muscles when sitting upright and did not relax in slumped sitting;
  - a biomechanical model classified 96.4% of cases correctly.

  — [KU Leuven Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/80558); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/13412/)
- **O'Sullivan K et al. 2012 (Ergonomics), systematic review of 7 high-quality studies** (5 crossover, 2 RCTs; PEDro-rated). No evidence supports dynamic sitting (e.g., dynamic chairs) as a stand-alone approach to managing LBP. — [UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effect-of-dynamic-sitting-on-the-prevention-and-management-of/)
- **Contrasting evidence: 2025 scoping review of office workers (J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil; 22 studies, n=7,814; 77% rated high quality).**
  - It concluded that longer sitting time, "poor sitting posture", fewer breaks and more static sitting were associated with LBP.
  - Counts by exposure: sitting time, 8 studies found a relationship and 5 did not; posture, 7 studies found a relationship; sitting behaviour, 12 found a relationship and 1 did not.
  - "The strongest evidence for an association with LBP was found for sitting behavior."
  - Caveat: a scoping review does not grade causal evidence.

  — [PubMed 40111906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40111906/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10538127251320320)

### Inferences
- A "correct posture" detector has no validated target. No reference sitting posture has been shown to predict pain prospectively, so any absolute "upright" threshold would encode a belief that clinicians hold (Korakakis) rather than an evidence-based criterion.
- Because LBP subgroups deviate in opposite directions (Dankaerts), a one-direction instruction such as "sit up straighter" could raise protective muscle activity in some people. This supports the app's design principle of never prescribing a correction direction.
- The evidence moves the monitoring target away from the *shape* of a posture and toward the *exposure pattern* (how long and how static). That is what option D measures.
- The 2025 scoping review's "poor posture" association conflicts with Swain, Roffey and Laird. It is best treated as hypothesis-generating, because cross-sectional associations cannot tell whether posture causes pain or pain changes posture (Bontrup 2019 raises this reverse-causation possibility; see Q3).

### Gaps
- The full texts of Swain 2020 and Roffey 2010 could not be accessed, so AMSTAR ratings per review and Bradford Hill item scores were not extracted.
- No prospective study was found testing whether webcam-detectable slouching predicts new-onset LBP in office workers.
- The designs of the studies in the 2025 scoping review (cross-sectional vs prospective) could not be verified.

## 2. Forward head posture (FHP) and neck pain: what do meta-analyses find, and is the evidence prospective?

### Takeaway
The association between forward head posture (measured as the craniovertebral angle, CVA) and neck pain is cross-sectional, small and age-dependent. Adults with neck pain show a few degrees more FHP and modest correlations with pain and disability; adolescents and older adults show no difference; and "text neck" was not associated with neck pain in young adults. Prospective office-worker studies identify history, sex, low task variation, workstation and psychosocial factors more consistently than head posture. One prospective cohort did link a very high proportion of sitting time, and (as a trend) prolonged neck flexion, to neck pain. For neck pain, the evidence-based help is exercise (strengthening), not posture correction.

### Cited Findings
- **Mahmoud et al. 2019 (Curr Rev Musculoskelet Med), systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies, 13 used in pooling** (secondary sources; primary blocked). Results:
  - pooling all ages, there was no statistically significant FHP difference between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups (7 studies);
  - after studies of adolescents or elderly participants were removed, adults with neck pain differed by **4.84° (95% CI 0.14–9.54)** in FHP;
  - statistically significant correlations were reported between FHP and neck pain intensity, and between FHP and Neck Disability Index scores;
  - the included studies compared symptomatic with asymptomatic groups at a single time point.

  — [Dovepress J Pain Res review citing Mahmoud](https://www.dovepress.com/therapeutic-exercise-for-forward-head-posture-in-neck-pain-patients-a--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-JPR); [1HP blog (secondary, non-peer-reviewed)](https://1-hp.org/blog/hpforgamers/the-great-forward-head-posture-debate/)
- **Damasceno et al. 2018 (Eur Spine J 27(6):1249–54), 150 participants aged 18–21.** No association between neck posture while texting (whether judged by physiotherapists or self-perceived) and neck pain or its frequency. Limitations: small sample and narrow age range. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs00586-017-5444-5)
- **Ariëns et al. 2001 (Occup Environ Med 58(3):200), prospective cohort of 1,334 workers in 34 companies over 3 years, with exposure measured from video.**
  - There was a significant positive relation between the percentage of working time spent sitting and neck pain, with increased risk above **>95% of working time seated**.
  - There was a *trend* toward more neck pain with **neck flexion ≥20° for >70% of working time**.
  - No clear relation was found for neck rotation.

  — [OEM](https://oem.bmj.com/content/58/3/200); [PMC1740110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1740110)
- **Jun et al. 2017 (Int Arch Occup Environ Health), systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 papers** (10 prospective cohorts and 2 RCTs). Risk factors for new neck pain in office workers were low satisfaction with the workplace environment, keyboard positioned close to the body, **low work task variation**, and self-perceived medium/high muscular tension. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00420-017-1205-3); [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/physical-risk-factors-for-developing-non-specific-neck-pain-in-of/)
- **Paksaichol et al. 2012 (Occup Environ Med 69(9):610), systematic review of prospective cohorts of office workers.** Only a history of neck pain and female sex emerged as likely risk factors. — [OEM](https://oem.bmj.com/content/69/9/610); [PainScience summary](https://www.painscience.com/biblio/only-a-history-of-neck-pain-and-being-a-woman-are-likely-risk-factors-for-neck-pain.html)
- **Chen et al. 2018 (Phys Ther), systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 workplace RCTs.**
  - There is moderate-quality evidence that neck/shoulder strengthening exercises and general fitness training reduce neck pain in symptomatic office workers.
  - The effect was larger for strengthening, and greater participation in exercise gave greater effects.
  - Ergonomic interventions were supported only by low-quality evidence.

  — [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/32398aae-fcdb-496c-8ef5-b23db68ad6a3); [MyOrthoEvidence](https://www.myorthoevidence.com/AceReport/Report/10086)
- **Hoe et al. 2018 (Cochrane CD008570, 15 studies).** An arm support used with a computer mouse "may or may not" prevent work-related upper limb or neck musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) in office workers. The effectiveness of other physical, organisational and cognitive ergonomic interventions remains uncertain. — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008570.pub3/information)
- A 2023 scoping review titled "Defining text neck" exists, indicating that the construct itself lacks a settled definition. Its content was not reviewed. — [PubMed 37405530](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37405530/)

### Inferences
- The FHP–neck pain link is cross-sectional, so direction cannot be established: pain could change posture as easily as posture could cause pain. A ~5° adult group difference is small relative to normal within-person variation over a workday. There is no evidence that steering head posture toward a threshold prevents neck pain.
- A front-facing webcam cannot measure true CVA, which requires a lateral view of the C7 landmark. Front-view proxies (face-size change, nose-to-shoulder-line distance, head pitch) are a further step removed from the validated measure.
- The exposure measures with prospective support are *duration-based*: sitting for >95% of time, neck flexion sustained for >70% of time, and low task variation. These fit option D (monitoring time and variation) better than option A (absolute angle thresholds).
- Suggested activities with the best evidence for neck pain are strengthening and fitness exercise (moderate quality), not posture correction.

### Gaps
- The primary Mahmoud 2019 text could not be accessed. Exact correlation coefficients, risk-of-bias ratings and the elderly/adolescent subgroup numbers were not obtained.
- No prospective study was found showing that "text neck" or webcam-measured head-forward position predicts future neck pain.

## 3. Posture variability and static loading: is prolonged static sitting a risk factor, and what is behind "the best posture is the next posture"?

### Takeaway
Evidence that static sitting or low variation is harmful is suggestive but mostly cross-sectional or mechanistic. The strongest signals are:
1. Prospective data: low task variation predicts neck pain, and sitting for >95% of working time is associated with neck pain.
2. A cluster RCT in which a sensor-driven device prompting active breaks or postural shifts reduced new-onset neck pain and LBP.
3. Office studies in which static sitting behaviour is the exposure most consistently associated with LBP.

"The best posture is the next posture" is an ergonomics maxim with no single traceable originator and no direct trial behind it.

### Cited Findings
- **Bontrup et al. 2019 (Appl Ergon 81), 64 call-centre workers; sitting behaviour recorded with a textile pressure mat over ~400 hours in total.**
  - 75% reported some chronic or acute back pain.
  - Workers with chronic LBP showed a *non-significant trend* toward more static sitting.
  - The association with sitting behaviour was greater for chronic than for acute pain. The authors suggest this is plausibly because people with chronic pain are more aware of pain-free sitting positions, which raises the possibility of reverse causation.

  — [ETH Research Collection](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/354215)
- The 2025 office-worker scoping review found sitting behaviour (fewer breaks, more static sitting) to be the most consistently associated exposure: 12 of 13 studies. — [PubMed 40111906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40111906/)
- In prospective data, low work task variation predicts new neck pain — [Jun 2017, Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00420-017-1205-3) — and sitting for >95% of working time is associated with neck pain — [Ariëns 2001, OEM](https://oem.bmj.com/content/58/3/200).
- **Srinivasan & Mathiassen 2012 (Clin Biomech 27:979–993), review.** Motor variability (natural variation in postures, movements and muscle activity) is "a relevant issue in occupational health and performance". Regularly altering the movement pattern has been suggested as a way to avoid overloading the same tissues. The review mainly sets out research questions, so this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated effect. — [Record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4026471)
- **Waongenngarm et al. 2021 (Scand J Work Environ Health 47(4)), 3-arm cluster RCT in 6 Bangkok organisations; n=193 healthy but high-risk office workers; 6-month follow-up.**
  - Arms: active break (n=47), postural shift (n=46) and control with a placebo seat pad (n=100).
  - The device combined a seat pad, a processor and a smartphone app. It collected sitting duration, break duration and number of postural shifts, then *personalised* its recommendations for active breaks or postural shifts.
  - New-onset neck pain: 17% (active break), 17% (postural shift), 44% (control).
  - New-onset LBP: 9% (active break), 7% (postural shift), 33% (control).
  - Adjusted hazard ratio indicating a protective effect for neck pain: **HRadj 0.45 (95% CI 0.20–0.98)**.
  - The device was designed on the premise that sitting for longer than 30 minutes possibly increases the risk of neck pain and LBP.

  — [PMC8091075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8091075/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f8888fe44b57471da828dd5d24595c79)
- Earlier laboratory work by the same group, as cited in search extractions: during 1 hour of sitting, a postural-shift frequency of 20–30 shifts per hour gave significantly lower perceived discomfort in the neck, shoulder, upper back and low back than 10 shifts per hour. Secondary mention; primary not opened. — [PMC8091075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8091075/); [Chulalongkorn repository](https://cuir.car.chula.ac.th/handle/123456789/44367)
- "The best posture is the next posture" circulates in trade and ergonomics media as part of a shift away from "sit up straight" toward movement and a range of postures. No single originator was identified. — [EHS Today](https://www.ehstoday.com/news/ehs_imp_32786)
- Dynamic sitting equipment alone is not supported for LBP. — [O'Sullivan K 2012, UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effect-of-dynamic-sitting-on-the-prevention-and-management-of/)

### Inferences
- Two signals have better, though still modest, evidentiary support than any threshold on posture *shape*:
  - "static too long": no meaningful change of position for N minutes;
  - "time since last break".

  A webcam combined with keyboard and mouse activity can measure both without judging posture.
- The 2021 RCT is the closest real-world analogue to this app, since it used sensor-driven, personalised prompts. Its small number of clusters (6), very high incidence in the control group and seat-pad sensor (not a webcam) limit how far it generalises. It also tested *prompting variation and breaks*, not correction toward a target posture.
- Progressive slouching within a sitting bout may be a proxy for accumulating fatigue or discomfort. There is no evidence that the slouch itself is harmful, so it is better used as a cue to "change position or move" than flagged as an error.
- The 20–30 shifts per hour lab finding implies that normal comfortable sitting involves frequent small shifts. A "stillness" alert should therefore fire only after a substantially longer absence of shifts. The exact threshold is not evidence-defined and would need empirical tuning.

### Gaps
- No trial was found that isolates postural-shift prompts from break prompts in general (not high-risk) office workers. No evidence was found on what magnitude of position change counts as "meaningful".
- The Waongenngarm et al. 2020 Applied Ergonomics study of discomfort versus postural shifts during 4-hour sitting could not be retrieved. Arm-specific hazard ratios, including those for LBP, from the 2021 RCT were not obtained.
- Static muscle-load ("Cinderella") hypotheses and microbreak EMG studies such as McLean 2001 were not retrieved.
- No source was retrieved showing that posture progressively degrades within a sitting bout (creep) in office workers.

## 4. Breaks and sit–stand: what frequency and duration are supported, and what are the effects on discomfort and productivity?

### Takeaway
Overall, the certainty of the break evidence is low to very low (Cochrane 2019). Even so:
- There is **moderate-quality evidence that active breaks with postural change reduce discomfort and pain**, and that breaks do not hurt productivity (Waongenngarm 2018).
- NIOSH field studies show that a 5-minute supplementary break every hour reduces discomfort and eyestrain with no loss of output.
- Sit–stand desks reliably cut sitting time by about 1.5–2 hours a day (very-low-certainty evidence), but the evidence that they reduce musculoskeletal symptoms is insufficient, and prolonged standing is itself associated with LBP.
- Guideline break intervals cluster around 30–60 minutes. No study tests 40 minutes specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **Luger et al. 2019 (Cochrane CD012886): 6 RCTs, 373 workers, interventions lasting 1 day to 6 months.** Extractions state that "six studies investigated work-break frequencies, two investigated work-break types, and one investigated both". Certainty of evidence was low to very low. Findings:
  - additional work breaks "may not have a considerable effect on musculoskeletal pain, discomfort or fatigue" compared with no additional breaks;
  - active breaks (e.g., stretching) or cognitive breaks may have little to no effect on musculoskeletal fatigue compared with passive breaks (very uncertain);
  - very-low-quality evidence that different break frequencies *may* positively affect productivity;
  - certainty was downgraded because studies were small, delivered breaks in different ways, and relied on self-reported outcomes.

  — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012886.pub2/information); [PMC6646952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6646952/); [Unbound Medicine](https://evidence.unboundmedicine.com/evidence/view/Cochrane/438467/all/Work%E2%80%90break_schedules_for_preventing_musculoskeletal_symptoms_and_disorders_in_healthy_workers)
- **Waongenngarm et al. 2018 (Appl Ergon), systematic review of 8 RCTs and 3 non-randomised trials (10 rated high quality), graded with GRADE.**
  - Low-quality evidence of a conflicting effect of breaks on pain.
  - Low-quality evidence of a positive effect on discomfort.
  - **Moderate-quality evidence of a positive effect of active breaks with postural change on pain and discomfort.**
  - **Moderate-quality evidence that breaks have no detrimental effect on work productivity.**
  - Conclusion: active breaks with postural change may reduce pain in acute LBP and prevent discomfort in healthy people.

  — [PubMed 29409639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/29409639); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/52797)
- **Galinsky et al. 2000 (NIOSH field study of data-entry operators).** Workers alternated in 4-week blocks between a conventional schedule (two 15-minute breaks) and a supplementary schedule that added **a 5-minute break in each hour without a break**, totalling 20 extra minutes per day.
  - Discomfort in several body areas, and eyestrain, were significantly lower on the supplementary schedule.
  - The weekly build-up of right forearm, wrist and hand discomfort was eliminated.
  - Data-entry performance did not fall.

  — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/198356)
- **Galinsky et al. 2007 follow-up.** Discomfort and eyestrain were again lower, and supplementary breaks slowed their accumulation during work. Data-entry speed was faster, so output was maintained despite 20 minutes less work time. — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286)
- **Albulescu et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE), meta-analysis of micro-breaks: 22 samples, N=2,335.**
  - Vigour increased (d=0.36, p<.001).
  - Fatigue decreased (d=0.35, p<.001).
  - Overall performance did not change significantly (d=0.16, p=.116); a performance benefit appeared only for less cognitively demanding tasks.
  - Longer breaks gave a larger performance boost.

  — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- **Shrestha et al. 2018 (Cochrane CD010912; 34 studies, 3,397 participants).**
  - Sit–stand desks reduced workplace sitting by **84–116 min/day** (very-low-quality evidence).
  - They also reduced total sitting and the number of sitting bouts lasting ≥30 minutes.
  - No harms were observed (musculoskeletal pain, varicose veins, lost productivity).
  - Most evidence was low quality.

  — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010912.pub5/information); [Cochrane featured review](https://www.cochrane.org/news/featured-review-workplace-interventions-reducing-time-spent-sitting-work)
- **Parry et al. 2019 (Cochrane CD012487; 10 studies, 955 workers with musculoskeletal complaints).** Sit–stand workstations compared with no intervention:
  - short-term (<6 months) LBP intensity: SMD −0.35 (95% CI −0.80 to 0.10; 2 RCTs; low quality), not significant;
  - medium-term pain-related disability: MD −0.4 (−2.70 to 1.90; 1 RCT; low quality), not significant;
  - the evidence is insufficient to show that sit–stand or treadmill desks reduce low or upper back symptoms.

  — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012487.pub2/information); [PMC6953379](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6953379)
- **Coenen et al. 2018 (BJSM 52(3):176–183), 49 studies (45 cross-sectional, 5 longitudinal; n=88,158).** Substantial occupational standing is associated with low-back symptoms, and inconclusively with lower-extremity symptoms. The conclusion is tentative because there were few high-quality longitudinal studies. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/3/176)
- **Buckley et al. 2015 (BJSM 49(21):1357), expert statement.**
  - Desk workers should initially accumulate **2 h/day of standing and light activity** during working hours, progressing to **4 h/day**.
  - Seated work should be broken up with standing work, sit–stand desks or short active standing breaks.
  - The evidence base is largely observational or retrospective, plus short-term cardiometabolic studies.

  — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/49/21/1357)
- **Cornell (Alan Hedge) "20-8-2" guidance:** sit 20 minutes, stand 8 minutes, move or stretch 2 minutes, repeating every 30 minutes. Over a 7.5-hour day this is about 5 h sitting, 2 h standing and 0.5 h moving, with 16 sit–stand changes. The primary Cornell page could not be fetched. Vendor blogs' claims about its evidence base (for example, "published in BJSM", or standing still for more than 10 minutes causing blood pooling) could not be verified. It appears to be expert guidance rather than a trial-derived schedule. — [Cornell Ergonomics (primary; not accessible here)](https://ergo.human.cornell.edu/CUESitStandPrograms.html); [UPLIFT Desk blog (vendor)](https://www.upliftdesk.com/blog/20-8-2-rule-standing-desk/); [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2211299/standing-desk-20-8-2-rule-explained/)
- **Korean Ministry of Employment and Labor guideline for VDT (computer display) work (영상표시단말기 취급근로자 작업관리지침):** no continuous work bout longer than 1 hour, with **10–15 minutes of rest per hour** of continuous work. — [KoreaScience PDF](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO200913060412507.pdf); [KLRI record](https://klri.re.kr:9443/handle/2017.oak/6753?mode=full)
- **Exercise for LBP prevention: Steffens et al. 2016 (JAMA Intern Med; 21 RCTs, 30,850 participants).**
  - Exercise plus education reduced the risk of an LBP episode: RR 0.55 (95% CI 0.41–0.74), moderate quality.
  - Exercise alone: RR 0.65 (0.50–0.86), low to very low quality.
  - Education alone had no effect: RR 1.03 (0.83–1.27).

  — [Macquarie University](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/prevention-of-lowback-pain-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/); [chiro.org abstract](https://chiro.org/LINKS/ABSTRACTS/Prevention_of_Low_Back_Pain.shtml)

### Inferences
- Break reminder content should centre on changing position, standing and walking briefly, which is the "active break with postural change" with moderate-quality support. Stretch suggestions are low-risk optional content, but the Cochrane review found no clear advantage of active (stretching) over passive breaks for fatigue (very uncertain). Stretches should not be framed as corrective.
- **40-minute reminder:** this falls inside the 30–60-minute range implied by Cornell's 30-minute cycle, the Waongenngarm device's 30-minute premise, the Korean guideline's 60-minute limit and NIOSH's hourly 5-minute breaks. No evidence distinguishes 40 minutes from 30 or 60. The interval should be user-configurable. A two-tier design is consistent with the evidence:
  - frequent micro-prompts (about 1–2 minutes of position change or standing);
  - hourly or so 5–15-minute breaks.
- Productivity worries are not supported by the evidence: breaks show no detrimental effect on productivity (moderate quality), and NIOSH found output maintained.
- Standing is not a cure. Prolonged standing is associated with LBP, so prompts should encourage *alternating* rather than "stand more".
- Regular exercise outside the app (strengthening and general fitness) has better preventive evidence than any in-app posture feature. A neutral pointer to it is reasonable for a non-medical app.

### Gaps
- No trial comparing break intervals (for example 30 vs 40 vs 60 minutes) on musculoskeletal outcomes in office workers was found.
- No peer-reviewed trial of the Cornell 20-8-2 schedule was found, and the primary Cornell text could not be accessed.
- Long-term (>12 months) effects of break-reminder software on pain were not found.
- How the micro-break studies in Albulescu 2022 defined a "micro-break" (maximum duration) was not verified.

## 5. Lateral asymmetry (leaning, crossed legs, uneven weight bearing), leg-length discrepancy, and self-correction before diagnosis

### Takeaway
Evidence linking habitual asymmetric sitting to pain or spinal asymmetry is limited to small cross-sectional or laboratory studies. Anatomic leg-length inequality (LLI) is nearly universal: the mean is about 5 mm, and it is usually not clinically significant below about 20 mm. Pelvic obliquity or torsion has not been shown to relate to LBP, and postural habits do not appear to cause scoliosis. No evidence was found supporting self-correction of perceived asymmetry before diagnosis. Because LBP subgroups deviate in opposite directions and comfortable postures vary between people, a self-chosen correction direction could be wrong.

### Cited Findings
- **Cross-legged sitting laboratory study (IJERPH 2020;17(13):4621), 30 people with LBP and 30 healthy controls.**
  - Compared with erect sitting, cross-legged sitting produced greater pelvic obliquity, more lumbar and thoracic kyphosis, more posterior pelvic tilt and a lower trunk flexion angle.
  - People with LBP showed greater asymmetry of buttock pressure and more slouching.
  - The authors *hypothesise* that prolonged, habitual cross-legged sitting "may cause" LBP or deformity. They also note benefits of crossing the legs (reduced muscle fatigue, sacroiliac joint stability).
  - The design is cross-sectional, so causation was not tested.

  — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/13/4621); [PMC7370107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7370107/)
- **Knutson 2005 (Chiropr Osteopat 13:11), review based on accurate X-ray methods.**
  - Prevalence of anatomic LLI: **90%**.
  - Mean magnitude: **5.2 mm (SD 4.1)**.
  - For most people, LLI does not appear clinically significant until about **20 mm**.
  - Conclusion: anatomic LLI is near universal, but the average magnitude is small and unlikely to be clinically significant.

  — [PMC1232860](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1232860/)
- **Leg-length discrepancy (LLD) and the pelvis.** The evidence reviewed indicates that neither pelvic torsion nor pelvic unlevelling/obliquity is related to LBP. In people with LLD up to 22 mm, the pelvis typically twists (pelvic torsion), with the hip bone rotating forward on the shorter-leg side. These statements are attributed via search extraction; the primary review was not opened. — [Cureus 2024 review PDF](https://assets.cureus.com/uploads/review_article/pdf/232344/20240417-4512-1rdtwmj.pdf)
- **Systematic review of LLD and back pain** (9 studies from MEDLINE): 3 positive, 4 negative and 2 with no conclusion. — [WSO library record](https://library.wso.at/s/orw/item/2270)
- **Campbell et al. 2017, systematic review of shoe lifts.** There is *low-quality* evidence that shoe lifts reduce pain and improve function in people with LLD and common painful musculoskeletal conditions. High-quality research is still needed to define what threshold of LLD should be corrected, and how. — [wikiMSK PDF](https://wikiMSK.org/w/img_auth.php/f/f0/Shoe_lifts_for_leg_length_discrepancy_systematic_review_-_Campbell_2017.pdf)
- **Scoliosis:** among Portuguese adolescents, no association was found between adopting "incorrect" postures or carrying overweight backpacks and scoliosis. — [Work 2016 (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/WOR-162284)
- LBP subgroups deviate in opposite directions — [Dankaerts, Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/80558) — and "comfortable postures vary between individuals" — [Slater 2019, UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/sit-up-straight-time-to-re-evaluate/).

### Inferences
- The app should not label leaning left or right as an error, nor nudge the user toward a symmetric target. At most, a lateral lean could feed the *static-exposure* metric with a neutral description (e.g., "same position for 35 min"), with no directional advice.
- An optional private log of lean-side time could help the user discuss patterns with a clinician once the X-ray and leg-length results are available. If a clinician later prescribes a direction (for example, after a confirmed structural LLD or scoliosis), a clinician-set target could be supported, but that is outside a non-medical scope.
- Alternating sides or leg-crossing is consistent with the variation evidence. A sustained one-sided lean is better addressed as "static too long" than as "asymmetric".
- Self-reported symptoms such as one side of the low back rising when bending can come from many structural and non-structural causes. The research found nothing that lets a webcam distinguish between them. Whether the user's left–right features have a structural cause is for the pending diagnosis, not the app, to decide.

### Gaps
- No prospective study was found on habitual one-sided leaning or leg-crossing and incident LBP, and no RCT of correcting sitting asymmetry was found.
- No study was found on how accurately people perceive their own asymmetry, or on harms of self-correcting before diagnosis.
- No sitting-specific evidence on LLD was retrieved. (The mechanistic point that a seated pelvis rests on the sitting bones, so LLD mainly matters when standing, is not sourced here.)

## 6. Are anterior pelvic tilt (APT), rib flare and rounded shoulders reliably measurable and meaningfully linked to pain?

### Takeaway
APT is the *norm* in pain-free adults. Lumbar lordosis and standing pelvic tilt do not differ between people with and without LBP, and thoracic kyphosis does not differ between people with and without shoulder pain (moderate evidence). "Rib flare" has no standardised definition or validated measure in the literature found. None of these features has evidence of being a meaningful pain driver, and none can be measured reliably from a front-facing webcam (inference).

### Cited Findings
- **Herrington 2011 (Manual Therapy), 120 pain-free adults measured with a palpation meter:** 85% of men and 75% of women stood in anterior pelvic tilt; only 9% of men and 18% of women were neutral. Secondary source; primary not opened. — [E3 Rehab (secondary)](https://e3rehab.com/stop-trying-to-fix-anterior-pelvic-tilt/)
- **Laird 2014:** no difference in lordosis (8 studies) or in standing pelvic tilt between people with and without LBP. — [PMC4096432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4096432/)
- **Barrett et al. 2016 (Man Ther), 10 studies** (4 low, 3 moderate and 3 high risk of bias).
  - *Moderate* evidence of no difference in thoracic kyphosis between people with and without shoulder pain.
  - *Strong* evidence that maximum shoulder range of motion is greater in erect than slouched postures, but only from single-session studies.
  - Conclusion: "thoracic kyphosis may not be an important contributor to the development of shoulder pain."

  — [UHRA](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/5872/); [UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/is-thoracic-spine-posture-associated-with-shoulder-pain-range-of--2/)
- **Rib flare.** One education-provider article states that rib flare lacks a standardised definition, normative data and objective criteria. It describes a widened lower front rib cage as a mechanical consequence of extending the thoracic spine. No peer-reviewed measurement or reliability study of "rib flare" was found. — [Brookbush Institute (non-peer-reviewed)](https://brookbushinstitute.com/articles/rib-flare-assessment-the-myth-of-flared-ribs)
- **LUMOback wearable sensor study:** acceptable test–retest reliability for the sensor's posture score and sitting time, and preliminary evidence of more slouched lower-back/pelvis posture in people with prolonged LBP. The design is cross-sectional. — [PMC5606340](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5606340)

### Inferences
- "APT", "rib flare" and "rounded or internally rotated shoulders" are common clinical labels with weak validation as pain drivers. The app should neither detect nor name them, which also respects the no-diagnosis principle.
- A front-facing laptop or monitor webcam typically sees the head, shoulders and upper torso, and usually not the pelvis. Pelvic tilt, kyphosis and CVA are side-on (sagittal-plane) measures that need a lateral view. Shoulder protraction or rotation cannot be robustly inferred from a frontal 2D image.

### Gaps
- No evidence specific to *internal shoulder rotation* (as distinct from thoracic kyphosis or forward shoulders) and pain was found.
- No reliability or validity data were found for webcam-based frontal estimates of shoulder protraction, pelvic tilt or rib-cage position.

## 7. Monitor viewing distance and height: guideline values, the evidence behind them, and the 20-20-20 rule

### Takeaway
Guidelines converge on placing the monitor directly in front at roughly arm's length, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level and its centre about 15–20° below horizontal. The minimum distance is about 40–50 cm, but the upper limits differ:
- OSHA: 50–100 cm.
- CSA (Canada): 40–74 cm, reported via a secondary source.
- Korea's labour ministry: at least 40 cm.

The proposed 50–70 cm band is narrower than any of these. The evidence behind the recommendations is mostly laboratory and field ergonomics plus consensus. The 20-20-20 eye-break rule began as clinical advice without validation, and two small 2023 studies disagree on whether it helps.

### Cited Findings
- **OSHA Computer Workstations eTool.**
  - Preferred eye-to-screen distance is **20–40 inches (50–100 cm)**, and at least 20 inches.
  - The top line of the screen should be at or below eye level, with the screen centre normally **15–20° below horizontal eye level**.
  - A screen too far away leads to leaning forward and straining to read small text, eye fatigue, and loss of backrest support.
  - A screen too close makes the eyes work harder (convergence and focusing) and can prompt awkward postures such as tilting the head back.

  — [OSHA eTool](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/components/monitors)
- **Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS).**
  - Place the monitor about arm's length away, directly in front of or slightly to one side of the user.
  - The top of the screen should be no higher than eye level.
  - Position it about 15° below the horizontal line of sight, giving a preferred visual zone of ±15°.
  - The CSA office ergonomics guideline (Z412) recommends **400–740 mm** for prolonged viewing; this figure comes from a search extraction and CSA Z412 itself was not accessed.

  — [CCOHS monitor positioning PDF](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/ergonomics/office/CCOHS_monitor_positioning.pdf); [UNBC monitor guide](https://www.unbc.ca/sites/default/files/sections/safety/monitor.pdf)
- **Korean Ministry of Employment and Labor VDT work guideline:** the line of sight should be level with the top of the screen, and the eye-to-screen distance should be **at least 40 cm**. — [KoreaScience PDF](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO200913060412507.pdf); [KLRI record](https://klri.re.kr:9443/handle/2017.oak/6753?mode=full)
- **ANSI/HFES 100-2007 and ISO 9241-5.** Search extractions gave inconsistent numbers that could not be verified: "400–800 mm"; "50–70 cm"; "1.5–2.5 × screen height"; "at least 40 cm". The sources were low-quality or content-farm pages and a furniture vendor checklist, so none of these values should be relied on. — [ANSI webstore preview (not readable here)](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/HFES/preview_ANSI%2BHFES%2B100-2007.pdf)
- **Monitor placement field study** (Psihogios/Sommerich research group, as extracted). Viewing angles of 0° and −17.5° to the screen centre were compared. Monitor placement affected head tilt but not trunk posture, and total discomfort was greater at 0°. — [Scholarhub record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/ac325ce3-c589-403f-87ed-c0bc046780be); [IOS Press (Work)](https://content.iospress.com/articles/work/wor00801)
- **Origin of the 20-20-20 rule:** optometrist Jeffrey Anshel created it in the early 1990s, adapting evidence on short, frequent breaks for musculoskeletal disorders to the visual system. For years no peer-reviewed study had validated it. — [Optometry Times](https://www.optometrytimes.com/view/behind-the-creation-of-the-20-20-20-rule-for-digital-eye-strain)
- **Talens-Estarelles et al. 2023 (Cont Lens Anterior Eye), 29 symptomatic computer users.**
  - Bespoke laptop software used the **webcam** to track breaks, eye gaze and blinking, and gave personalised 20-20-20 reminders for 2 weeks.
  - Behaviour changed: more breaks per day, shorter computer-work stretches and shorter breaks.
  - Digital eye strain and dry-eye symptoms decreased, but the improvement was **not maintained** one week after the reminders stopped.
  - No binocular vision changes occurred except increased accommodative facility.
  - The design was an uncontrolled before-and-after study.

  — [Aston University](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effects-of-breaks-on-digital-eye-strain-dry-eye-and-binocular/)
- **Johnson & Rosenfield 2023 (Optom Vis Sci 100(1):52–56), 30 young adults doing a 40-minute demanding tablet reading task on four occasions.** Little to no evidence that the 20-20-20 rule is effective; longer breaks or a different frequency may help more. — [NZ Optics](https://www.nzoptics.co.nz/live-articles/20-20-20-rule-ineffective); [Edinburgh letter to the editor](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/letter-to-the-editor-20-20-20-rule-are-these-numbers-justified/)
- Hourly 5-minute supplementary breaks reduced eyestrain in NIOSH field studies (see Q4). — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/198356)

### Inferences
- A strict 50–70 cm band would flag setups that OSHA (up to 100 cm) and the Korean guideline (≥40 cm) consider acceptable. It would be better to flag only sustained clear outliers (e.g., under ~40 cm) and to present distance as setup information rather than a continuous alert.
- Comfortable viewing distance depends on screen size and text size. A webcam estimate of distance (from face width or the distance between the pupils) also carries error, so no single number fits all.
- The vertical angle a webcam sees mixes head pitch with camera and screen placement. Screen-height advice is better delivered as a one-time setup checklist than inferred continuously.
- Eye-break evidence is mixed. Folding eye breaks into the movement or break prompts (as NIOSH breaks did) reduces alert fatigue compared with a separate 20-minute nag. Talens-Estarelles shows that webcam-based reminder software is feasible and changes break behaviour, but the benefit faded once the reminders stopped.

### Gaps
- The primary texts of ANSI/HFES 100-2007, ISO 9241-5 and CSA Z412 were not accessible (paywalled or blocked); their numeric values remain unverified.
- No prospective evidence was found that following monitor height or distance guidance reduces neck pain. The Hoe 2018 Cochrane review rates the evidence for workstation ergonomic interventions as uncertain.
- The primary Korean guideline text (law.go.kr) was not retrieved. Its values (≥40 cm; top of screen at eye level; 10–15 minutes of rest per hour) come from secondary Korean academic PDFs.

## 8. Possible harms of posture monitoring and "fix your posture" messaging (fear-avoidance, catastrophizing, pain)

### Takeaway
No study was found that directly measured whether posture-monitoring apps cause fear, catastrophizing or vigilance. However, credible expert consensus and indirect evidence suggest that "bad posture" and "protect your spine" messages can foster fear, protective guarding and unhelpful beliefs, whereas reassuring messages are associated with better outcomes. The two device trials point the same way:
- In a sham-controlled trial, audio feedback on posture did *not* change posture behaviour.
- In another trial, a device that prompted *breaks and postural shifts* (variation rather than correctness) reduced the onset of pain.

### Cited Findings
- **O'Sullivan P et al. 2020 (BJSM):** the 10 facts (including "Back pain is not caused by poor posture" and "Pain with exercise and movement doesn't mean you are doing harm") target unhelpful beliefs that influence behavioural and psychological responses to pain. Clinicians are asked to build these facts into their interactions with patients. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/12/698.full)
- **Slater 2019 (JOSPT):** common warnings to protect the spine are not evidence-informed and can lead to fear. — [UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/sit-up-straight-time-to-re-evaluate/)
- **Nocebo and fear-avoidance:**
  - Believing that there are harmful or unsafe ways to move can act as a nocebo, leading to fear-avoidance and maladaptive movement strategies.
  - In chronic LBP, pain-related fear is associated with reduced lumbar range of motion (protective guarding), which may carry pro-nociceptive consequences that keep pain going.
  - A common belief is that the back is fragile and must be protected.

  — [Physiotutors research summary](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/safety-of-bending-with-low-back-pain/)
- **Reassuring messages:** in a cohort with chronic LBP, greater awareness of key reassuring messages at 3 months positively influenced pain intensity, disability and fear-avoidance at 6 months. Secondary summary. — [chiro.org summary](https://chiro.org/Graphics_Box_LOW_BACK/Does_Awareness_of_Key_Reassuring_Messages.shtml)
- **ELF trial (Ribeiro et al. 2020, Occup Environ Med 77(7):462), participant- and assessor-blinded, sham-controlled cluster RCT at 19 sites with 130 healthcare workers.** A device gave audio feedback whenever forward bending of the lower back and pelvis passed a threshold. It did **not** reduce the number of threshold exceedances: there was no change within groups at 1 week and no difference from sham. — [OEM](https://oem.bmj.com/content/77/7/462); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/61426)
- **Waongenngarm 2021:** a device prompting active breaks and postural shifts reduced 6-month new-onset neck pain and LBP compared with a placebo seat pad (see Q3). — [PMC8091075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8091075/)
- **Korakakis 2019:** 93.9% of physiotherapists consider posture education important. "Ideal posture" messaging is therefore common in practice, even though the evidence does not support it. — [UL Pure](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/physiotherapist-perceptions-of-optimal-sitting-and-standing-postu/)

### Inferences
- Copy should describe *time and variation*, not judgements:
  - Use: "You've been in one position for 40 min — a good moment to move."
  - Avoid: "bad posture", "slouching", "your pelvis is tilted", "fix your shoulders".
- Avoid red or alarm states and "posture scores" that imply a correct posture, especially for a user who already notices several perceived postural faults and is awaiting diagnosis, a situation in which vigilance about the body could plausibly increase.
- Include occasional reassurance consistent with O'Sullivan 2020, for example: "No single posture is right or wrong; changing position is what helps."
- Make user-reported comfort or discomfort the primary feedback metric rather than a computed posture score. Cap prompt frequency to limit alert fatigue and body-vigilance (plausible, not directly tested).

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measured fear-avoidance, catastrophizing or hypervigilance resulting from webcam or wearable posture monitoring or apps.
- A nocebo video study by Demoulin et al. (Eur Rehab J 2022) surfaced in searches but was not reviewed — [ORBi PDF](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/300075/1/02.2023_Demoulin%20et%20al.%20Eur%20Rehab%20J.%202022_nocebo%20effect%20video.pdf).

## 9. (From the objective) What this implies for which signals the app should monitor: options A, C and D, the 40-minute reminder, and stretches

### Takeaway
The evidence favours option D: sitting-bout duration, time since the last break, stillness or low positional variation, and within-bout drift used only as a "time to move" cue. It also supports break reminders whose content emphasises postural change, standing or walking. Option C (a personal baseline) is useful as a *reference for detecting change and variation*, not as a "correct" target. Option A (absolute population thresholds) has the weakest support and the highest nocebo risk. It should be limited to coarse, informational workstation-setup checks such as clear viewing-distance outliers. Lateral asymmetry should not be prompted at all before diagnosis.

### Cited Findings
- No posture causes or prospectively predicts LBP; there is no consensus on causality. — [Swain 2020, ACU](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8wq8w/no-consensus-on-causality-of-spine-postures-or-physical-exposure-and-low-back-pain-a-systematic-review-of-systematic-reviews); [Roffey 2010 via IASP](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/relief-news/article/back-pain-myths-closing-sale-everything-must-go/)
- Exposure *pattern* has the most consistent associations and prospective support:
  - static sitting behaviour — [2025 scoping review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40111906/);
  - sitting for >95% of working time — [Ariëns 2001](https://oem.bmj.com/content/58/3/200);
  - low task variation — [Jun 2017](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00420-017-1205-3).
- A sensor-driven intervention that prompted breaks and postural shifts reduced new pain in an RCT. — [Waongenngarm 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8091075/)
- Active breaks with postural change have moderate-quality evidence for reducing discomfort and pain without loss of productivity. — [Waongenngarm 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/29409639)
- Audio feedback on a posture threshold did not change behaviour compared with sham. — [ELF trial](https://oem.bmj.com/content/77/7/462)
- Guideline break intervals run from 30 to 60 minutes:
  - 60 minutes with 10–15 minutes of rest — [Korean VDT guideline](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO200913060412507.pdf);
  - hourly 5-minute breaks — [NIOSH](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/198356);
  - a 30-minute cycle — [Cornell 20-8-2 (vendor-reported)](https://www.upliftdesk.com/blog/20-8-2-rule-standing-desk/).
- Exercise (strengthening and fitness) is the intervention with moderate-quality evidence for neck pain — [Chen 2018](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/32398aae-fcdb-496c-8ef5-b23db68ad6a3) — and for preventing LBP — [Steffens 2016](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/prevention-of-lowback-pain-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/).

### Inferences
Suggested signal priorities follow, inferred from the findings above.

1. **Option D (primary).**
   - (a) *Sitting-bout timer*: face present and/or keyboard or mouse activity, with breaks detected from absence or idle time. Prompt at a user-configurable interval (default 40 minutes is defensible as within 30–60, but is not evidence-specific).
   - (b) *Stillness / low variation*: no meaningful change in head or shoulder position for a long stretch. Prompt "change position" as a micro-break.
   - (c) *Within-bout drift*: gradual change relative to the user's own *start-of-bout* position. Show it as a gentle cue that a shift or break may be due, never as an error, and preferably aggregated rather than as real-time nags.
2. **Option C (supporting).** Learn a personal baseline to normalise across camera setups and to quantify *variation*, not to define "good". Re-learn it periodically, because healthy sitting involves frequent shifts.
3. **Option A (minimal).**
   - Use only for one-time or occasional *setup* checks with broad, guideline-consistent ranges, for example flagging only a sustained distance under about 40 cm, and screen-top height advice delivered as a checklist.
   - Do not use absolute head-tilt, shoulder-tilt or slouch-ratio thresholds as alerts. These have no validated pain-predictive cut-offs, and shoulder tilt or lean would implicitly enforce symmetry before diagnosis.
4. **Asymmetry.** Do not prompt. Optionally keep a private, neutral log (for example, time leaning to each side) that the user can choose to show a clinician after the X-ray and leg-length assessment.
5. **Break content.** Prioritise standing, walking or changing position. Offer stretches as optional, non-corrective variety (Cochrane found no clear benefit of stretching over passive breaks). Include a neutral note that regular strengthening and fitness exercise has the best preventive evidence. Avoid exercise prescriptions targeting the user's perceived faults (pelvic tilt, rib flare, shoulder rotation).
6. **Eye breaks.** Fold "look away into the distance" into the movement prompts rather than running a separate 20-minute timer, because the 20-20-20 evidence is mixed and alert fatigue is a concern.
7. **Measurement of success.** Track adherence to breaks and self-rated comfort, not a "posture score".

### Gaps
- No evidence-based threshold exists for "stillness" (minutes without a meaningful shift) or for "meaningful" change magnitude; empirical tuning will be needed.
- No study has compared break-reminder intervals or messaging framings (neutral vs corrective) on pain, adherence or fear outcomes in office workers.
- No validation was found of webcam-based estimates of posture variation against reference measures.
