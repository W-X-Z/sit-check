## 1. Posture biofeedback wearables (Upright GO, Lumo Lift, vibrotactile shirts/sensors): posture angles, pain and adherence over weeks; do effects persist after feedback stops?

### Takeaway
Wearable vibrotactile or audio posture feedback reliably changes posture **while it is switched on**, mostly in single-session lab studies. There is little multi-week evidence on adherence. There is almost none showing that posture gains persist after feedback stops. The largest controlled pain trial (RESTORE, n=492) found that adding wearable movement-sensor biofeedback to cognitive functional therapy gave **no extra benefit**. Motor-learning research predicts this pattern: continuous concurrent feedback improves performance during practice but creates dependence on the feedback.

### Cited Findings
**Short-term effects (while feedback is on)**
- [S] In a single session of prolonged computer typing, a wearable biofeedback sensor that "can be set up to vibrate whenever the user slouches" significantly reduced neck flexion, thoracic kyphosis and pelvic plane angles compared with the same task without the sensor. The authors limit the claim to helping people keep an upright sitting posture "during a single session of prolonged computer typing" — [IJERPH 2021, 18(10):5430](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18105430); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8161121/)
- [S] In adults with non-specific neck pain, a commercially available wearable real-time biofeedback device "improves sitting posture and reduces muscular activity … during computer work". These are immediate effects only. The authors say future studies should examine long-term effects for preventing and managing neck pain — [PubMed 30359957](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30359957/)
- [S] A rapid review covers exactly this question: "effectiveness and use of wearable biofeedback motion capture systems in ergonomics to mitigate adverse postures and movements of the upper body". Its full text could not be opened here; it is the first source to check for retention data — [PMC11175029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11175029/)
- [K] In a field study with order pickers, smart workwear gave real-time vibrotactile feedback on trunk inclination and arm elevation. It reduced time spent in high-risk postures while feedback was active. The study also looked at the period after feedback was removed, but those numbers could not be re-checked — [Lind et al. 2020, Applied Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Reducing+postural+load+in+order+picking+through+a+smart+workwear+system%22)
- [K] In a lab study, a clip-on posture-correction sensor reduced head and neck flexion at both sitting and standing workstations (verify the size of the effect) — [Ailneni et al. 2019, Work (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Influence+of+the+wearable+posture+correction+sensor+on+head+and+neck+posture%22)
- [K] A systematic review of spinal-posture wearables found that most devices were validated as measuring instruments in labs. Few studies tested clinical outcomes or long-term use — [Simpson et al. 2019, BMC Musculoskelet Disord (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22The+role+of+wearables+in+spinal+posture+analysis%22)

**Pain outcomes**
- [K] RESTORE (Lancet 2023) was a 3-arm phase-3 RCT with 492 people with chronic disabling low back pain. Cognitive functional therapy (CFT) alone and CFT plus wearable movement-sensor biofeedback each reduced activity limitation compared with usual care at 13 weeks (RMDQ mean difference ≈ −4.6 points in both arms, verify). The effects lasted to 52 weeks. Biofeedback gave **no added clinical benefit** over CFT alone — [Kent et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00441-5)
- [K] An earlier pilot cluster RCT tested the same type of motion-sensor biofeedback (ViMove) against guideline-based care and reported larger improvements in activity limitation and pain. So the benefit seen against a weaker comparator disappeared in a head-to-head test against an active therapy that targets beliefs — [Kent, Laird & Haines 2015 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22changing+movement+and+posture+using+motion-sensor+biofeedback%22)
- [K] The evidence that posture causes pain is weak. A systematic review of systematic reviews found no consensus that spinal postures cause low back pain — [Swain et al. 2020, J Biomech (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22No+consensus+on+causality+of+spine+postures+or+physical+exposure+and+low+back+pain%22). In 18–21-year-olds, neck flexion while texting was not associated with neck pain — [Damasceno et al. 2018, Eur Spine J (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Text+neck+and+neck+pain+in+18-21-year-old+young+adults%22)

**Adherence over weeks (wearables)**
- [K] LUMOback is a belt-worn posture and sitting tracker that vibrated on slouching and prolonged sitting. It was the device arm of a cluster RCT in office workers (organisational strategies with vs without the tracker). A companion mixed-methods paper reported usage over time and acceptability. Its usage-decline figures could not be re-opened (see Gaps) — [Brakenridge et al. 2016, IJBNPA (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22organisational-level+strategies+with+or+without+an+activity+tracker%22); [Brakenridge et al. 2018, Interact J Med Res (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Usage+Acceptability+and+Effectiveness+of+an+Activity+Tracker+in+a+Randomized+Trial+of+a+Workplace+Sitting+Intervention%22)
- [K] A lumbopelvic monitor-and-feedback device that alerts on excessive or sustained lumbar flexion was tested in workers, first in a feasibility RCT and then in the ELF cluster RCT. The outcomes could not be re-checked — [Ribeiro et al. 2014, JOSPT (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22lumbopelvic+monitor+and+feedback+device+to+change+postural+behavior%22)

**Persistence after feedback stops**
- [K] Under the "guidance hypothesis", frequent concurrent feedback improves performance during practice, but learners come to rely on it. Retention, meaning performance after the feedback is withdrawn, then suffers — [Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.95.3.355)
- [K] Giving feedback less often (faded, roughly 50% of trials) led to similar learning but **better delayed retention** than feedback on every trial — [Winstein & Schmidt 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.677). "Bandwidth" feedback, given only when the error exceeds a tolerance, also supports learning — [Sherwood 1988 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Effect+of+bandwidth+knowledge+of+results+on+movement+consistency%22)
- [K] A review of augmented visual, auditory and haptic feedback in motor learning found that concurrent feedback works very well for immediate performance, and haptic feedback is especially good for spatial or postural aspects. Concurrent feedback risks dependency, while reduced, delayed or faded schedules support retention better — [Sigrist et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0333-8)
- [S] Recent designs are moving from slouch alarms toward "dynamic sitting reminders", which encourage movement and variation rather than holding a target posture — [Smart garment for dynamic sitting reminders, Sensors 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25113359)
- [S] RCTs of wearable posture feedback for neck pain are still registered or under way, so definitive outcome data are pending. Examples: wearable plus phone app plus exercise in university students, and wearable postural feedback plus exercise in office workers with chronic neck pain — [NCT07763327](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07763327); [registry listing](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/the-wearable-postural-feedback-devices-combined-with-exercise-in-office-workers-with-chronic-neck-pa)

### Inferences
- Expect the camera alerts to change head and neck angles **in the minutes after an alert**. Do not expect, or claim, that posture correction by itself will reduce pain. RESTORE and the weak posture–pain evidence both argue for framing the app around movement, variety and comfort rather than "correct posture".
- Concurrent feedback creates dependence, so a coach aiming for lasting change should do three things. (a) Fire only when the user is outside a tolerance band and the condition has persisted; the existing 90 s persistence rule already does this. (b) Fade alert frequency over weeks. (c) Shift over time from in-the-moment alerts to factual summaries after the fact.
- For wearables, the burden of wearing the device dominates adherence. A desktop camera app avoids that burden but takes on detection false alarms and camera/privacy concerns. That makes false-alarm control the main adherence lever (see section 4).
- Two signals under consideration fit the "movement variety" framing better than absolute angle thresholds do: prolonged stillness, and gradual slouch measured against the user's own posture at the start of the sitting bout. They also avoid comparing the user to an "ideal" posture.

### Gaps
- No RCT with multi-week posture or pain outcomes could be found or verified specifically for **Upright GO or Lumo Lift**. The device brand used in the IJERPH 2021 study and in PubMed 30359957 was not visible in the snippets.
- Quantitative retention after feedback removal (Lind 2020, the ELF trial, the rapid review) and the LUMOback usage-decline percentages could not be opened. These are the most important numbers to verify.

## 2. HCI studies of desktop/webcam posture feedback: ambient vs interruptive feedback, acceptance and annoyance

### Takeaway
HCI posture-feedback papers repeatedly conclude that immediate, interruptive posture alarms break concentration and are often ignored. This has pushed designs toward ambient, peripheral, gradual or even sub-perceptual cues: avatars, light, projection, and monitors that move slowly. The evidence is mostly short lab sessions or small deployments. **No multi-week head-to-head comparison** of ambient vs interruptive desktop posture feedback was found.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Haller et al. (INTERACT 2011) built an office chair that senses posture and tested three alert modalities: graphical, physical and vibrotactile feedback. The aim was to find "the most effective interruption modality without causing disruption" — [Haller et al. 2011 (PDF)](https://dl.ifip.org/db/conf/interact/interact2011-2/HallerRBGSSNSI11.pdf)
- [S] PosProjector states that "traditional posture correction systems use immediate feedback to notify users of wrong posture, [but] they often disrupt users' concentration". It explores ambient projected notifications during desk-based study — [PosProjector (ACM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791156)
- [S] A flower-shaped ambient avatar was motivated by the observation that earlier posture notifications are "perceived as intrusive and tend to be ignored". The physical avatar reflects posture "subtly and pleasantly" — [ACM, 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2677199.2680575)
- [S] "Slow Robots for Unobtrusive Posture Correction" (CHI 2019, KAIST) — [paper PDF](http://library.usc.edu.ph/ACM/CHI2019/1proc/paper613.pdf). [K] The concept: a monitor moves more slowly than people can perceive, so users follow it and change posture without any alert. The effect sizes could not be re-checked.
- [S] A human-factors evaluation compared an ambient display for real-time posture feedback with an on-screen display and with no display, under dual-task conditions. It measured detection efficiency and acceptance (visibility, understandability, performance expectancy, effort expectancy) — [ResearchGate (c. 2020)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347630796_Human_Factors_Evaluation_of_an_Ambient_Display_for_Real-Time_Posture_Feedback_to_Sedentary_Workers)
- [S] A CHI 2024 literature review covers both sitting-posture recognition and posture *feedback* — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642657)
- [S] A September 2026 preprint proposes "cognition-aware posture feedback", which adapts feedback to the user's cognitive state — [ErgoAssist, arXiv 2609.00440](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00440)
- [S] Notification intensity can be designed as a scale "from subtly drawing attention up to forcing the user to take action" — [arXiv 1808.01745](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.01745)
- [K] Webcam self-modelling: office workers who received periodic webcam photos of their own posture, together with ergonomic guidance, improved their RULA posture scores more than controls (verify group sizes and follow-up length) — [Taieb-Maimon et al. 2012, Applied Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22self-modeling+webcam+photos%22+musculoskeletal+office+workers)
- [K] Visual and vibrotactile feedback for seated posture guidance have been compared directly. Which one was less disruptive should be verified before quoting — [Zheng & Morrell 2013, IEEE Trans Haptics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Comparison+of+visual+and+vibrotactile+feedback+methods+for+seated+posture+guidance%22)
- [K] Ambient displays for sitting and posture include:
  - Breakaway, a desk sculpture that slumps the longer the user sits — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Breakaway%22+%22ambient+display+designed+to+change+human+behavior%22)
  - perFrames, picture frames whose photo reacts to posture — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22perFrames%22+persuasive+picture+frames+posture)
  - "Make me move at work!", an ambient light display — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Make+me+move+at+work%22+ambient+light+display)
  - Health Bar, an ambient display — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22The+health+bar%22+persuasive+ambient+display)
  - LightSit, a pressure-sensing cushion with ambient light for posture and microbreaks — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22LightSit%22+unobtrusive+health-promoting+system)
  - "calm persuasive technology" at work — [lookup](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22calm+persuasive+technology%22+wellbeing+at+work)

  These were typically small or short deployments that report good acceptance and low disruption rather than large, durable behaviour effects.
- [K] As a precedent for the breathing icon: a peripheral (visual or auditory) paced-breathing cue during information work lowered breathing rate without measurably harming task performance — [Moraveji et al. 2011, UIST "Peripheral paced respiration" (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Peripheral+paced+respiration%22)
- [K] Interrupting mid-task is costly. When peripheral tasks interrupted a primary task instead of appearing at a task boundary, users needed **3–27% more time**, made **twice as many errors**, reported **31–106% more annoyance** and showed **twice the increase in anxiety** — [Bailey & Konstan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.12.009)
- [K] A notification costs attention even when the user does not act on it — [Stothart et al. 2015 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22The+attentional+cost+of+receiving+a+cell+phone+notification%22)

### Inferences
- The app's non-focus-stealing card already sits between ambient and modal. The literature supports a **staged** design. Step 1 is an ambient cue (menu-bar icon state). Step 2 is the card, shown only if the condition persists **and** the user is at a breakpoint. A modal or sound should never be used for posture.
- Effects that cover the whole screen, such as blur, dimming or tint, are attention-grabbing and directly interfere with work. Use them sparingly or not at all; no comparative evidence either way was found.
- The webcam self-modelling result suggests that factual, self-referenced information can work without evaluative labels, e.g. "head about 9 cm closer than at 10:05". Showing actual photos raises privacy and self-consciousness concerns (inference).

### Gaps
- The results of Haller 2011 (which modality worked best or disrupted least), the effect sizes in the Slow Robots paper, the ambient-display human-factors study and the CHI 2024 review could not be read.
- No peer-reviewed multi-week evaluation of consumer webcam posture apps (macOS or Windows menu-bar utilities) was found.

## 3. Trials of break-reminder software and stand/move prompts: compliance, sitting time, discomfort, productivity; compliance decay

### Takeaway
On-screen prompts reliably produce **small** reductions in sitting. A 2025 meta-analysis found −12.5 min of sitting and +1,030 steps per workday over a median of 8 weeks. Older microbreak trials showed less discomfort **without productivity loss**. Prompts mainly shorten long uninterrupted bouts rather than total sitting. Long-term evidence is sparse. In the best-quantified prompting trial (HeartSteps), the effect of each prompt faded over the weeks even though prompts continued.

### Cited Findings
**Sitting time and steps**
- [S] A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of computer prompts ("automated, on-screen notifications that remind users to interrupt prolonged sitting by standing, stretching, or engaging in brief movements") pooled **18 studies with 1,164 office workers**. Results: sitting **−12.46 min/workday**; steps **+1,029.99/workday**; median intervention length **8 weeks** (range 1–24). The authors say longer prospective studies of work and health outcomes are needed — [IJBNPA 2025 (PMC12164069)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12164069/); [PubMed 40514667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40514667)
- [K] Point-of-choice prompting software (every 30 min, verify) plus education was compared with education only (n≈28, 5 days). The prompts reduced the **number and duration of sitting events lasting more than 30 min** but did not significantly change total sitting time — [Evans et al. 2012, Am J Prev Med (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Point-of-choice+prompts+to+reduce+sitting+time+at+work%22)
- [S] Point-of-choice prompts have also been tested with sit-stand workstation users (2018). The results could not be opened — [PMC6258737](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6258737/)
- [K] B-MOBILE was a within-subjects study (n≈30 adults with overweight or obesity). Smartphone prompts after 30, 60 or 120 min of continuous sedentary time asked for a 3-, 6- or 12-min activity break. All three schedules reduced sedentary time. The frequent, short schedule (3 min per 30 min) performed best (verify) — [Bond et al. 2014, PLoS One (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22B-MOBILE%22+smartphone-based+intervention+to+reduce+sedentary+time)
- [K] A secondary analysis of moment-by-moment responses to the B-MOBILE prompts was used to inform JITAI optimisation — [Thomas & Bond 2015, Health Psychology (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Behavioral+response+to+a+just-in-time+adaptive+intervention%22+sedentary)
- [K] HeartSteps was a micro-randomized trial: n=37, 6 weeks, 5 decision points per day, with a 0.6 probability at each point of sending a walking or an anti-sedentary suggestion. Suggestions raised steps in the following 30 min by **≈14%** on average. Most of the effect came from walking suggestions; anti-sedentary suggestions had little effect. The effect **declined with days in the study** — [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067)
- [K] Technology-enhanced sedentary-behaviour interventions (computer, mobile, wearable) pooled to ≈ −41 min/day of sedentary time, with larger effects in short-term studies (verify) — [Stephenson et al. 2017, IJBNPA (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22wearable+technology+enhanced+interventions+to+reduce+sedentary+behaviour%22). Interventions using self-monitoring gave a small but significant reduction — [Compernolle et al. 2019, IJBNPA (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Effectiveness+of+interventions+using+self-monitoring+to+reduce+sedentary+behavior+in+adults%22)
- [K] Cochrane rates the evidence that computer prompting reduces workplace sitting as low quality and inconsistent. Sit-stand desks have the most consistent evidence — [Shrestha et al. 2018 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Workplace+interventions+for+reducing+sitting+at+work%22)

**Discomfort, pain and productivity**
- [K] Microbreaks during computer work reduced discomfort. The benefit was clearest with breaks **every 20 min**, compared with every 40 min or at the worker's discretion, and productivity did not drop — [McLean et al. 2001, Applied Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Computer+terminal+work+and+the+benefit+of+microbreaks%22)
- [K] Break-prompting software (breaks only, or breaks plus exercises) was used for 8 weeks by 268 computer workers with neck or upper-limb complaints. Both software groups reported recovery or improvement more often than controls. The effect on severity was unclear, and productivity was not reduced (verify the odds ratios and the productivity direction) — [van den Heuvel et al. 2003, Scand J Work Environ Health (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22software+programs+stimulating+regular+breaks+and+exercises%22)
- [K] For data-entry operators, supplementary rest breaks (four extra 5-min breaks) reduced discomfort and eyestrain without lowering productivity — [Galinsky et al. 2000, Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22A+field+study+of+supplementary+rest+breaks+for+data-entry+operators%22)
- [K] Alternating sitting and standing every 30 min over a work week reduced fatigue and lower-back discomfort compared with seated work, with no change in productivity — [Thorp et al. 2014, Occup Environ Med (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Breaking+up+workplace+sitting+time+with+intermittent+standing+bouts%22)
- [K] A systematic review found that breaks, especially ones involving a change of posture, may reduce discomfort or low back pain without harming productivity — [Waongenngarm et al. 2018, Applied Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22effects+of+breaks+on+low+back+pain%22+office+workers+systematic+review). Cochrane rates the evidence on work-break schedules as very low to low certainty — [Luger et al. 2019 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Work-break+schedules+for+preventing+musculoskeletal+symptoms+and+disorders+in+healthy+workers%22)

**Compliance and decay**
- [K] In HeartSteps (above), the effect of each prompt decays over weeks even while prompting continues. This is the clearest quantified evidence of habituation to activity prompts.
- [K] Information workers' responses to a break-prompting system depend strongly on work context such as meetings and focused work — [Luo et al. 2018, CHI "Time for Break" (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Time+for+Break%22+%22break+prompting+system%22). Another approach times prompts to natural work breaks — [Cambo et al. 2017, CHI "BreakSense" (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22BreakSense%22+work-breaks)
- [S] The 2025 meta-analysis notes that long-term prospective data are lacking (median duration 8 weeks) — [PMC12164069](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12164069/)

### Inferences
- The app's **40-min** sitting trigger is within the tested range (prompts at 30–60 min). The microbreak and B-MOBILE data suggest that shorter, more frequent, briefer breaks are at least as beneficial, but each extra prompt adds burden (section 4). A reasonable compromise: an ambient icon cue at about 30 min and the card at about 40–45 min (inference).
- The most responsive outcome is **ending a long bout**, not total sitting time (Evans 2012). The proximal success metric should therefore be "sitting bout broken within ~10 min of the card". A "Done" click is a weaker measure. HeartSteps used a 30-min window for the same purpose.
- Plan for modest, decaying effects. A weekly completion target of 50% or more is plausible early on but will likely erode without fading and variation (see sections 4 and 6).
- Productivity concerns about short breaks are not supported by the trials. The real productivity cost comes from **when** the prompt interrupts (Bailey & Konstan 2006), not from the break itself.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed effect estimates for **smartwatch stand reminders** (Apple Watch Stand, Fitbit "Reminders to move") could be found or verified.
- Quantified compliance-decay curves for desktop break software were not verified. Likely sources are Henning et al. 1997 (two field sites, short hourly breaks) and Slijper et al. 2007 (logs from pause-software use); neither could be opened.
- Swartz et al. 2014 (prompts to stand vs to step) and Pedersen et al. 2014 (Exertime, prompts every 45 min) are relevant prompt trials whose results could not be verified.

## 4. Alert fatigue and habituation: notification frequency, false-positive tolerance, snoozing, interruptibility, and JITAI design principles

### Takeaway
People respond to alerts roughly in proportion to how often the alerts are right ("probability matching"). False alarms damage both responding to alerts and trusting their absence, so they do more harm than misses. Below about **70% reliability**, an automated aid performs worse than no aid. Each additional or repeated alert lowers acceptance. Habituation to identical warnings sets in within a few exposures; varying their appearance slows it. Deferring alerts to task boundaries reduces annoyance and errors. JITAI design says to intervene only when the person is both in need **and** receptive, and to treat "no intervention" as a valid option. No study gives a numeric count of false alarms at which users disable alerts. The degradation is graded.

### Cited Findings
**Reliability, false alarms and trust**
- [K] A synthesis of imperfect diagnostic automation found a crossover at **≈0.70 reliability**. Below it, automated alerts are worse for performance than no automation — [Wickens & Dixon 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/14639220500370105)
- [K] Automation prone to false alarms degraded both **compliance** (acting on alerts) and **reliance** (trusting the system when it is silent). Automation prone to misses mainly reduced reliance. False alarms are therefore the more damaging error type — [Dixon, Wickens & McCarley 2007, Human Factors (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22On+the+independence+of+compliance+and+reliance%22)
- [K] Probability matching: people tend to respond to alarms at roughly the rate at which the alarms turn out to be true — [Bliss, Gilson & Deaton 1995, Ergonomics (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Human+probability+matching+behaviour+in+response+to+alarms%22)
- [K] Automation errors on **easy** cases, which users can judge for themselves, undermine trust disproportionately — [Madhavan, Wiegmann & Lacson 2006, Human Factors (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Automation+failures+on+tasks+easily+performed+by+operators%22)
- [K] Trust falls after salient faults and recovers only gradually — [Lee & Moray 1992 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Trust+control+strategies+and+allocation+of+function+in+human-machine+systems%22). Trust is calibrated by making clear how and why the automation acts — [Lee & See 2004](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392). Explaining why an aid errs changes trust and reliance — [Dzindolet et al. 2003 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22The+role+of+trust+in+automation+reliance%22)
- [K] Clinical decision support:
  - Drug-safety alerts are overridden in **49–96%** of cases — [van der Sijs et al. 2006, JAMIA](https://doi.org/10.1197/jamia.M1809)
  - **72–99%** of clinical monitor alarms are false, which leads to desensitisation — [Cvach 2012 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Monitor+alarm+fatigue%22+integrative+review)
  - In primary care, the probability of accepting a reminder fell by **≈30% for each additional reminder per encounter**. It fell by **≈10% for each 5-percentage-point rise in the share of repeated reminders**. Desensitisation from repeats mattered more than workload (verify the exact figures) — [Ancker et al. 2017, BMC Med Inform Decis Mak](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8)

**Habituation and volume**
- [K] fMRI, eye-tracking and field studies show that neural and behavioural habituation to repeated identical warnings develops within a few exposures. "Polymorphic" warnings, whose appearance varies, reduced habituation — [Vance et al. 2018, MIS Quarterly (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Tuning+out+security+warnings%22); [Anderson et al. 2015, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22How+polymorphic+warnings+reduce+habituation+in+the+brain%22)
- [K] In an in-situ study of phone notifications, users received ≈63.5 notifications per day. A higher volume was associated with more negative emotion — [Pielot, Church & de Oliveira 2014, MobileHCI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2628363.2628364)
- [K] Batching notifications (about 3 times a day) improved well-being and attention compared with immediate delivery. Receiving no notifications at all increased anxiety and fear of missing out — [Fitz et al. 2019 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Batching+smartphone+notifications+can+improve+well-being%22)
- [K] Prompts and reminders generally have small positive short-term effects on engagement with digital interventions. The optimal frequency is not established — [Alkhaldi et al. 2016, JMIR (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22effectiveness+of+prompts+to+promote+engagement+with+digital+interventions%22); [Muench & Baumel 2017, JMIR, on prompt ("trigger") design and habituation (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22More+than+a+text+message%22+dismantling+digital+triggers)

**Timing and interruptibility**
- [K] Interrupting mid-task vs at a boundary: 3–27% more time, twice the errors, 31–106% more annoyance and twice the anxiety increase — [Bailey & Konstan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.12.009)
- [K] Interruptions at coarse breakpoints produced less annoyance, frustration and time pressure than interruptions at other moments — [Adamczyk & Bailey 2004, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22If+not+now+when%22+interruption+at+different+moments+within+task+execution)
- [K] Deferring notifications until breakpoints in the user's task reduced frustration and the time needed to resume the task, compared with immediate delivery — [Iqbal & Bailey 2008, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Effects+of+intelligent+notification+management+on+users+and+their+tasks%22)
- [K] Simple sensors predicted office workers' self-reported interruptibility, and the most useful was whether anyone was talking, along with keyboard and mouse activity. Models reached ≈82% accuracy against ≈77% for human observers (verify) — [Hudson et al. 2003, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Predicting+human+interruptibility+with+sensors%22)
- [K] Delivering notifications at detected breakpoints in device use reduced perceived mental effort compared with immediate delivery — [Okoshi et al. 2015, UbiComp (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Reducing+users+perceived+mental+effort+due+to+interruptive+notifications%22)
- [K] Receptivity to notifications depends on how relevant the content is and on the current task. Interruptions during complex tasks are perceived as more disruptive — [Mehrotra et al. 2016, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22My+phone+and+me%22+receptivity+to+mobile+notifications)
- [K] A status light driven by computer interaction data (keyboard and mouse) reduced interruptions from colleagues in a field deployment (≈46%, verify). This shows that inferring flow from keyboard and mouse activity is feasible and valued — [Züger et al. 2017, CHI "FlowLight" (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22FlowLight%22+traffic+light+reduces+interruptions)
- [K] People compensate for interruptions by working faster, at the cost of more stress and frustration — [Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22The+cost+of+interrupted+work%22)

**JITAI design principles**
- [K] A JITAI has these components: **decision points, intervention options (including "provide nothing"), tailoring variables, decision rules, proximal outcomes and a distal outcome**. Support should be given only when the person is in a **state of vulnerability or opportunity** *and* in a **state of receptivity**, meaning able and willing to engage. Unnecessary prompts add burden and promote habituation and disengagement — [Nahum-Shani et al. 2018, Ann Behav Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-016-9830-8)
- [K] Micro-randomized trials randomize whether to intervene at each decision point. This estimates the time-varying effect on the proximal outcome and shows whether the effect wears off — [Klasnja et al. 2015, Health Psychology (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Microrandomized+trials%22+just-in-time+adaptive+interventions)
- [K] In HeartSteps the effect of a suggestion declined over weeks (section 3). The later reinforcement-learning version explicitly modelled "dosage", a discounted count of recent suggestions, to capture burden and habituation — [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067); [Liao et al. 2020, IMWUT "Personalized HeartSteps" (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Personalized+HeartSteps%22+reinforcement+learning)
- [K] Receptivity to mHealth prompts can be predicted from context such as time of day and current device use. Machine-learning-timed prompts increased receptivity compared with random timing — [Künzler et al. 2019, IMWUT (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Exploring+the+state-of-receptivity+for+mHealth+interventions%22); [Mishra et al. 2021, IMWUT (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Detecting+receptivity+for+mHealth+interventions+in+the+natural+environment%22)
- [K] A meta-analysis reported moderate-to-large JITAI effects (≈g 0.87 vs waitlist or no intervention; ≈g 0.41 vs non-JITAI treatment; verify) — [Wang & Miller 2020, Health Communication (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Just-in-the-moment+adaptive+interventions%22+meta-analytical+review). A systematic review of JITAIs for physical activity found few rigorous efficacy tests; most adaptation used time, location or activity — [Hardeman et al. 2019, IJBNPA (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22A+systematic+review+of+just-in-time+adaptive+interventions%22+physical+activity)

### Inferences
- **Express the false-alarm target as precision (PPV) per alert type, not as an absolute count.** If a posture alert type fires 4 times a day and 2 are false, PPV is 0.5, below the ≈0.70 crossover, and probability matching predicts that roughly half the alerts will be ignored. To keep PPV at 0.8 or above with 2 false alarms a day, about 8 true alerts a day would be needed, which is itself too many. A better target is "≥80% judged correct per type, and ≤1 false posture alert per day".
- **Alert volume under the current rules may be high.** In theory, three posture types each allowed once per 30 min, with 15 min between different types, allow up to about 4 posture cards per hour. Sitting cards add up to about 9–10 per 8-hour day. HeartSteps averaged only about 3 suggestions a day (5 decision points × 0.6) and still saw decay. **Add a global daily cap** on interruptive cards and **back off after cards are ignored or snoozed** (dosage-aware), e.g. lengthen the type's cooldown after each ignore. Exact caps are a design choice and need personal testing.
- **False alarms on "obviously fine" moments are the most damaging** (Madhavan 2006). Suppress posture cards when the posture is plausibly deliberate: reading close-up, leaning in to inspect something, during calls, or immediately after the user moves. Prefer **bout-relative** signals (stillness duration; gradual drift from the user's own bout-start baseline) over absolute angles.
- **The "was this alert right?" button** gives the per-type PPV needed for the 0.70/0.80 rule and supports trust calibration through transparency (Lee & See 2004).
  - Make it a one-click secondary control on the card, or ask only on a sample of cards, to limit burden.
  - Use the answers automatically. If a type's PPV is below 0.8, raise its persistence or threshold. If it stays below about 0.7 for two weeks, demote that type to ambient-only.
- **Timing:** hold cards until a breakpoint (evidence: Iqbal & Bailey; Adamczyk & Bailey; Hudson; Okoshi).
  - Deliverable moments on macOS: an app switch, an input pause after a typing burst, a window close, the end of a call, return from lock.
  - Defer during: active typing, camera or microphone in use by another app, screen sharing, full-screen presentation or video, and Focus/Do Not Disturb.
  - Set a maximum deferral (e.g. 5–10 min). Cancel the card if the condition resolves while it is being held.
- **Habituation:** keep the two-line structure stable but vary the content (rotate stretches, wording and visual accent), following the polymorphic-warning evidence. Avoid identical repeats within a day.
- **Snooze and mute** support autonomy and should stay (section 5). Treat rising snooze or mute rates as a fatigue signal that triggers backoff, not as non-compliance.
- **"Provide nothing" as a default option**, as in JITAI design: if the user has broken the sitting bout on their own, no card should appear.

### Gaps
- No direct study was found giving **how many false alarms** users tolerate before disabling a *wellness* app. The best available proxies are the human-factors evidence (probability matching, the ~0.70 crossover) and clinical alert-fatigue data.
- No quantitative evidence was found on **snooze behaviour**: how often reminders are snoozed, or whether snoozing predicts abandonment.
- The exact Ancker 2017, Hudson 2003, Wang & Miller 2020 and FlowLight figures are marked "verify" because they could not be re-opened.

## 5. Message framing: neutral vs evaluative or fear-based messages; autonomy-supportive prompts; does posture monitoring increase body vigilance, posture anxiety or pain beliefs?

### Takeaway
Controlling language ("must", "should") provokes reactance, and offering choice reduces it. Fear appeals work on average but mainly for one-off behaviours and only when the person feels able to act. Gain-framed messages have a small advantage for preventive behaviours. Clinical back-pain science has moved away from "sit up straight", because posture does not reliably cause pain and fear of posture can feed protective guarding. Quantifying an activity can erode intrinsic motivation. **No direct evidence** was found that posture monitoring increases body vigilance or posture anxiety. Adjacent evidence supports the project's neutral, single-action, non-scoring message rules: attention to the body increases symptom reports, anxiety about tracking has been documented for sleep trackers, and fitness trackers can create pressure and guilt.

### Cited Findings
- [K] Controlling language raised perceived threat to freedom and reactance. A "restoration of freedom" postscript (e.g. "it's your choice") reduced it, and concrete wording was more persuasive — [Miller et al. 2007, Human Communication Research](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x); [Dillard & Shen 2005, Communication Monographs (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22On+the+nature+of+reactance+and+its+role+in+persuasive+health+communication%22)
- [K] Gain-framed messages were more likely than loss-framed ones to change **prevention** behaviours, but the effect was small (r ≈ .08, verify). There was no framing effect on attitudes or intentions — [Gallagher & Updegraff 2012, Ann Behav Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-011-9308-7)
- [K] Fear appeals are effective on average (d ≈ 0.29). They work better when combined with efficacy statements and for **one-time** rather than repeated behaviours — [Tannenbaum et al. 2015, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039729). Threat is effective only when efficacy is high and can backfire when it is low — [Peters, Ruiter & Kok 2013, Health Psychology Review (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Threatening+communication%22+critical+re-analysis+fear+appeal)
- [K] "'Sit up straight': time to re-evaluate" (JOSPT 2019) argues that there is no single correct sitting posture and that upright posture is not protective against pain. Messages that make people fearful of "bad posture" may be unhelpful; varied, comfortable postures and movement are encouraged instead — [Slater et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2019.0610)
- [K] "10 facts every person should know about back pain" includes that back pain is not caused by poor posture, and that unhelpful beliefs and protective behaviours contribute to persistence — [O'Sullivan et al. 2020, BJSM](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-101611). CFT, which targets these beliefs, was effective in RESTORE, and adding sensor biofeedback did not improve it — [Kent et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00441-5)
- [K] Across six experiments, measuring an activity (e.g. step counting) increased how much people did but **reduced enjoyment** and later engagement, because measurement made the activity feel like work — [Etkin 2016, J Consumer Research](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv095)
- [K] After 8 weeks with fitness trackers, adolescents reported lower motivation and autonomy, along with feelings of pressure and guilt — [Kerner & Goodyear 2017 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22motivational+impact+of+wearable+healthy+lifestyle+technologies%22). "Orthosomnia" describes anxiety and worse sleep driven by sleep-tracker data — [Baron et al. 2017, J Clin Sleep Med](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.6472)
- [K] Focusing attention on internal bodily sensations increases symptom and fatigue reports — [Pennebaker & Lightner 1980, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.39.1.165). Hypervigilance to pain-related information is linked to fear and to the persistence of pain — [Crombez et al. 2005, Pain (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Hypervigilance+to+pain%22+experimental+clinical+analysis)
- [K] Self-determination theory: supporting **autonomy, competence and relatedness** is a design principle for motivation and well-being in digital products (the METUX model) — [Peters, Calvo & Ryan 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00797). Interventions informed by SDT have small-to-moderate effects on autonomous motivation and health behaviour — [Ntoumanis et al. 2021, Health Psychology Review (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22meta-analysis+of+self-determination+theory-informed+intervention+studies+in+the+health+domain%22)

### Inferences
- The project's rules (line 1 states facts only; line 2 gives one action; no evaluative or scoring language) match this evidence. They avoid controlling language that triggers reactance, avoid fear about posture, and avoid quantified scores that erode intrinsic motivation.
- Wording to avoid: "bad/poor posture", "slouching", "fix", "should/must", "warning", and anything implying damage or pain ("strain", "harm").
- For the proposed **progressive-slouch** signal, describe a change, not a fault. Example: "Head about 8 cm closer to the screen than at 10:05", not "you're slouching".
- Make the action an option. Examples: "Option: stand and roll your shoulders 5 times" or "If useful: …". Keep the snooze and mute buttons visible on the card, which acts as the "choice" restoration.
- Gain-framed or neutral consequences are acceptable but optional, e.g. "a 1-min stand often eases stiffness". Fear framing is not appropriate for a repeated, everyday behaviour.
- **Avoid a "posture score" or a streak that can be lost.** Given Etkin 2016 and the orthosomnia and tracker-guilt evidence, offer factual summaries (e.g. longest sitting bout, number of bouts over 40 min) and keep them optional.
- Users who already have neck or back pain may be most prone to vigilance. Low alert frequency and movement-oriented, non-alarming wording matter most for them (inference).

### Gaps
- No trial was found that directly compares neutral vs evaluative wording **for posture or break alerts**.
- No study was found that directly measures whether posture-monitoring devices or apps increase body vigilance, posture anxiety or pain catastrophising. This is an open research gap; the inferences above extrapolate from adjacent areas.

## 6. Why do people abandon posture apps and devices, and what supports long-term use?

### Takeaway
Abandoning self-tracking and mHealth tools is the norm rather than the exception. The main drivers are: burden (wearing, charging, interruptions), low perceived value or inaccuracy, discomfort, lack of fit with identity or routines, and goals being reached. Reminders raise short-term adherence but can **impede habit formation** by becoming a crutch. Long-term use is supported by low burden and high accuracy, cues tied to existing routines, tolerance of lapses and easy re-entry, user autonomy, and a gradual hand-over from the app to habit, which takes weeks to months.

### Cited Findings
- [K] An analysis of about 1,600 Craigslist ads selling health trackers found that people abandon them for varied reasons: goals reached, changed circumstances, unmet expectations, and the device not fitting their lives. Abandonment is not always failure — [Clawson et al. 2015, UbiComp (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22No+longer+wearing%22+abandonment+craigslist)
- [K] In a 2-month study, 17 participants given smart devices abandoned many of them. Reasons included data that wasn't useful, a mismatch with their self-image or routines, and maintenance burden — [Lazar et al. 2015, UbiComp (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Why+we+use+and+abandon+smart+devices%22)
- [K] Personal tracking involves **lapsing and resuming**, and designs should support re-entry — [Epstein et al. 2015, "lived informatics" model (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22A+lived+informatics+model+of+personal+informatics%22). Design should also support life after abandonment — [Epstein et al. 2016, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Beyond+abandonment+to+next+steps%22)
- [K] Reasons for abandoning wearable activity trackers include loss of motivation, usability and technical problems, wearing discomfort, data inaccuracy and privacy concerns — [Attig & Franke 2020, Computers in Human Behavior (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Abandonment+of+personal+quantification%22)
- [K] mHealth attrition:
  - pooled dropout ≈43% in app-based chronic-disease interventions (verify) — [Meyerowitz-Katz et al. 2020, JMIR (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Rates+of+attrition+and+dropout+in+app-based+interventions+for+chronic+disease%22)
  - median 15-day and 30-day retention of ≈4% and ≈3% for mental-health apps in real-world use (verify) — [Baumel et al. 2019, JMIR (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Objective+user+engagement+with+mental+health+apps%22)
  - the "law of attrition" — [Eysenbach 2005, JMIR](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.7.1.e11)
- [K] Reminders helped people repeat a behaviour but **hindered automaticity**. Cues tied to events or context, such as implementation intentions, supported habit formation better — [Stawarz, Cox & Blandford 2015, CHI (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Beyond+self-tracking+and+reminders%22). Dependence on reminders impedes habit formation — [Renfree et al. 2016, CHI EA (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Don%27t+kick+the+habit%22+dependency+habit+formation+apps). Digital behaviour-change interventions should target the automatic system and plan to withdraw scaffolding — [Pinder et al. 2018, ACM TOCHI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196830)
- [K] Habit automaticity took a median of **66 days** to reach 95% of its plateau, with a range of 18–254 days. Missing a single opportunity did not materially derail habit formation — [Lally et al. 2010, Eur J Soc Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- [K] Theories of maintaining behaviour change converge on five themes: maintenance motives (intrinsic and identity-related), self-regulation, habits, psychological and physical resources, and environmental or social context — [Kwasnicka et al. 2016, Health Psychology Review (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Theoretical+explanations+for+maintenance+of+behaviour+change%22)
- [K] The effect of each prompt decays over weeks — [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067). Quantification can erode enjoyment — [Etkin 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv095). Trackers can create pressure and guilt — [Kerner & Goodyear 2017 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22motivational+impact+of+wearable+healthy+lifestyle+technologies%22)
- [S] Recent ambient posture-feedback work is motivated by the observation that intrusive notifications are ignored — [ACM, 2015 flower avatar](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2677199.2680575); [PosProjector](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791156)

### Inferences
- For a camera-based desktop coach, the likely abandonment drivers are: false or obviously wrong alerts, interruptions during focused work or calls, repetitive identical cards, discomfort about camera privacy, and judgemental tone. These are inferences from the general evidence; no posture-app-specific study was verified.
- Long-term use is better supported by:
  - **accuracy** first (precision per type of 0.8 or more);
  - **a small daily dose** of well-timed cards;
  - **lapse-friendly design**: auto-pause during long absences and a guilt-free "welcome back" with no lost streaks;
  - **tying breaks to existing cues**, e.g. end of a meeting, after a build or commit, before getting coffee, with an optional "When X, I'll stand" plan;
  - **planned fading** after about 2–3 months, matching the habit-formation timescale (Lally 2010).
- **On-device processing and clear camera-state indication** are likely prerequisites for keeping the camera feature enabled. This is inference; no direct evidence was found.

### Gaps
- No verified data were found on usage curves or reasons for abandonment specifically for **Upright GO, Lumo Lift, LUMOback** (the Brakenridge 2018 figures could not be opened), or for **webcam posture apps**.
- No verified evidence was found on how camera-privacy concerns affect long-term use of posture apps.

## 7. (Objective) Design guidance for the macOS menu-bar posture coach, synthesised from sections 1–6

### Takeaway
The evidence favours a small number of high-precision, well-timed, neutrally worded cards, with ambient cues first. It also favours measuring how precise each alert type is and how often the user acts on it, backing off as fatigue appears, and fading prompts over weeks rather than escalating. The current design (non-focus-stealing card, 90 s persistence, cooldowns, snooze and mute, facts-then-action wording) is well aligned. The main risks are total alert volume, setting the false-alarm target as an absolute count, delivery at bad moments, and the lack of a fading or self-testing mechanism.

### Cited Findings (evidence anchors; details in sections 1–6)
- The ≈0.70 reliability crossover and the extra harm of false alarms — [Wickens & Dixon 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/14639220500370105); [Dixon et al. 2007 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22On+the+independence+of+compliance+and+reliance%22)
- Acceptance falls with each additional or repeated alert — [Ancker et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8)
- The cost of interrupting mid-task vs at a boundary — [Bailey & Konstan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.12.009); deferral to breakpoints — [Iqbal & Bailey 2008 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Effects+of+intelligent+notification+management+on+users+and+their+tasks%22)
- JITAI: vulnerability plus receptivity, "provide nothing", proximal outcomes — [Nahum-Shani et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-016-9830-8); prompt effects decay — [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067)
- Faded or bandwidth feedback gives better retention than continuous feedback — [Winstein & Schmidt 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.677); [Sigrist et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0333-8)
- Computer prompts have modest effects (−12.46 min/workday of sitting; +1,029.99 steps/workday) — [IJBNPA 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12164069/); microbreaks at about 20–30 min reduce discomfort without productivity loss — [McLean et al. 2001 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Computer+terminal+work+and+the+benefit+of+microbreaks%22); [Thorp et al. 2014 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Breaking+up+workplace+sitting+time+with+intermittent+standing+bouts%22)
- Varying warnings reduces habituation — [Vance et al. 2018 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Tuning+out+security+warnings%22)
- Reactance and choice — [Miller et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x); there is no single correct posture — [Slater et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2019.0610); costs of quantification — [Etkin 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv095)
- Reminders vs habit formation — [Stawarz et al. 2015 (lookup)](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Beyond+self-tracking+and+reminders%22); [Lally et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

### Inferences (concrete guidance; all are design inferences, not tested parameters)
1. **Tiered delivery.**
   - Tier 0: ambient. Change the menu-bar icon state, e.g. at about 30 min of sitting or when a posture condition begins.
   - Tier 1: the existing non-focus-stealing card, shown only when the condition persists (keep 90 s or more for posture, 40 min for sitting) **and** the user is at a breakpoint.
   - Never use modals or sounds for posture.
2. **Breakpoint gating on macOS.**
   - Hold the card during: active typing or mousing, camera or microphone in use by another app, screen sharing, full-screen apps, and Focus/Do Not Disturb.
   - Release it on: an app or window switch, a short input pause after a burst, the end of a call, unlock or wake.
   - Set a maximum hold of about 5–10 min. Drop the card silently if the condition resolves.
3. **Volume budget.**
   - Add a global daily cap on interruptive cards, e.g. 6–8 in total and 3 or fewer posture cards, as a starting point to tune.
   - Keep per-type cooldowns, and lengthen a type's cooldown each time its card is ignored or snoozed. This is "dosage" backoff, which HeartSteps modelled.
   - Merge co-occurring conditions into one card rather than sending sequential alerts.
4. **Reliability targets as precision.**
   - Replace "≤2 false alarms/day" with **≥80% judged correct per type** and **≤1 false posture card per day**.
   - Automatically demote to ambient-only any type whose rolling 2-week precision is below about 0.7.
5. **"Was this alert right?"**
   - Show it as a single secondary control on the card ("Not accurate"), or ask on a sample of cards only.
   - Each "not accurate" for a type raises that type's persistence (e.g. 90 → 120 → 180 s) or threshold.
   - Show the measured fact that triggered the card (value and duration), for transparency and trust calibration.
6. **Prefer bout-relative, non-judgmental signals.**
   - Prolonged stillness: "Same position for 35 min".
   - Gradual drift from the user's own bout-start baseline: "Head about 8 cm closer than at 10:05".
   - These suit the "movement variety" framing and are less likely to fire on obviously-fine postures than absolute-angle rules.
   - Suppress cards immediately after the user moves.
7. **Message template.**
   - Line 1, fact: "Seated 42 min".
   - Line 2, one optional action: "Option: stand and roll your shoulders 5 times".
   - Buttons: Done / 10 min / Mute 1 h / Not accurate.
   - Rotate actions and wording so no card repeats identically within a day.
   - Never use "bad/poor/slouch/fix/should/must", scores or streaks.
8. **Define completion objectively.**
   - Count "sitting bout broken within 10 min of the card" (person leaves the camera or input stops for 2 min or more, plus "Done").
   - Track it per alert type and per week.
   - Expect early weekly completion to fall over weeks (HeartSteps-style decay). If it falls below 50%, **reduce** volume and improve timing rather than escalating.
9. **N-of-1 self-experiment and fading.**
   - After a 2–3-week baseline, randomize about 30–50% of eligible decision points to "ambient only / no card".
   - Compare bout-breaking within 10 min between the two. This micro-randomized-trial logic for a single user estimates whether cards actually help this user and fades feedback at the same time, in line with the guidance hypothesis.
   - If cards add nothing over ambient cues, remove that card type.
10. **Breathing cue.** A static icon change every 20 min is cheap but low-salience and likely to be habituated to. Measure whether the user ever notices it (e.g. click-to-breathe opens a short paced animation). Peripheral pacing evidence (Moraveji 2011) concerns continuous pacing during work, not a static icon.
11. **Habit support.**
    - Offer optional cue-based plans ("When a meeting ends → stand 1 min") alongside the timers.
    - Make lapses cost nothing: no lost streaks, and auto-pause when the user is away.
    - Plan to fade cards after about 2–3 months if bouts stay short.
12. **Summaries over alarms.** Use an optional, factual end-of-day line (longest bout; number of bouts over 40 min; posture cards shown and how many were marked accurate) and no scores. This shifts feedback from concurrent to terminal, which supports retention, without evaluative framing.
